Two types of logistics leaders exist.

One makes the company more money by working less.
The other quietly enriches themselves at the company's expense.

If Pradyuman ever audited your vendor contracts — half your leadership team would be in the interrogation room.

── PART 1: THE SMART PLAY — Vendor Leverage ──

Most logistics leaders negotiate price. Smart ones engineer leverage.

Split $2M across 12 carriers → small account to all 12.
Consolidate into 2 carriers → strategic account to both.

Strategic accounts unlock:
→ Volume rebates (3–7% back on COGS)
→ Priority capacity when others scramble
→ Dedicated reps, not ticket queues
→ Rate locks and tech integrations first

A Louisiana contractor went from 8 vendors to 2:
- $42,000 saved
- Lead times down 3 days
- Margin: 12% → 18%

Walmart replaced 34 LTL shipments with one truckload — suppliers saved 20–30%. That's a leadership decision, not a procurement one.

Logistics = 8–14% of revenue. Fastest EBITDA lever most teams ignore.

5-step framework:
1. Audit dispersion — how many vendors split 80%+ of your spend?
2. Pick 1–2 strategic partners on volume, reliability, tech fit
3. Offer volume commitments for tiered rebates + SLA guarantees
4. Cut the tail — 40% fewer PO touches, 18 hrs/month less admin
5. Lock preferred status — you get the call when capacity tightens

── PART 2: THE AUDIT ROOM — How Leadership Bleeds the Company ──

ACFE 2024: 1,921 fraud cases. $3.1B in losses.
Executives = 27% of perpetrators. Median loss: $500,000 per case.
That's 3x managers. 8x employees.

How it happens in logistics:

1. Phantom vendors / shell companies
Payments approved to a vendor the leader secretly owns. Money cycles back.
→ Salt Lake Trucking siphoned $108M from FedEx over 10 years via shell companies.
→ Amazon delivery owner diverted $9.4M through fictitious vendors.

2. Vendor kickbacks
Contracts routed to a paying vendor. Kickbacks average 15–25% of contract value. The vendor inflates invoices to cover it — company absorbs both.

3. Inflated invoices
Vendor rates 40–100% above market. Excess flows back to the approving executive off-the-books.

4. Ghost employees
Non-existent headcount on payroll. Driver rosters and warehouse roles — high turnover, lax verification.

5. Expense misappropriation
Personal travel, luxury purchases billed as business expenses. Hardest to detect when the approver is the perpetrator.

Red flags: living beyond compensation, multiple properties, exotic travel, new vendors after leadership changes.

50% of occupational fraud involves corruption. Most lives inside the vendor relationships your leadership manages daily.

Pradyuman would find it in the first invoice.

Build systems that reward the first type of leader.
Expose the second before the audit does.
