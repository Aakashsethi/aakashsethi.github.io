The First Amendment doesn't protect your right to be heard. It protects the government's inability to silence you. That's a very different thing.

The First Amendment states: "Congress shall make no law... abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press."

Note the operative subject: Congress.

Isaiah Berlin, in Two Concepts of Liberty (Oxford, 1958, pp. 7–16), drew the defining distinction: negative liberty is freedom from coercive interference. Positive liberty is the actual capacity to act. The First Amendment is a masterwork of negative liberty. It was never designed to guarantee positive liberty — the capacity to speak, be heard, and participate in public discourse.

The First Amendment says nothing about:

→ A platform's right to remove or suppress your content
→ A corporation's right to decline to publish your story
→ An algorithm's right to deprioritize your reach below public visibility
→ An advertiser's right to withdraw funding when journalism becomes inconvenient

Tim Wu, in The Master Switch (Knopf, 2010, pp. 6–12), documents the recurring pattern he calls "the Cycle" — every major communications technology begins open, then consolidates under private control. Radio. Film. Cable television. The internet is not an exception. It is the latest iteration.

Yochai Benkler argued in The Wealth of Networks (Yale, 2006, pp. 1–28) that decentralised internet architecture could democratise the public sphere. That argument was correct in 2006. It is less correct after two decades of platform consolidation rebuilt gatekeeping at the infrastructure layer.

The relevant law:

Section 230 (47 U.S.C. § 230, 1996) — platform immunity from third-party content liability — has not been substantively amended in 28 years.

The EU Digital Services Act (Regulation 2022/2065) — requiring algorithmic transparency audits and systemic risk assessments — is the most rigorous platform governance framework in force. The U.S. has no equivalent.

Zeynep Tufekci, in Twitter and Tear Gas (Yale, 2017, pp. 237–266), identifies "algorithmic gatekeeping" — the capacity of platform architecture to shape which political realities become legible to public audiences. Censorship without a censor. Structural, invisible, constitutionally unaddressed.

The regulatory gap between where technology operates and where law reaches is one of the most significant accountability vacuums in contemporary governance.

Free media requires positive liberty — economic viability, distribution access, algorithmic transparency — without which the formal right to speak is functionally meaningless.

Those conditions do not currently exist at scale.
